## 1. The instrument and what it was meant to buy

Denmark did not persuade its citizens onto digital public services. It required
them. *Lov om Offentlig Digital Post* (LOV nr 528 af 11/06/2012) made a state-run
digital mailbox the default legal channel for public correspondence — compulsory
for businesses from 1 January 2013 and for every citizen aged 15 and over from
1 November 2014. Enrolment is automatic. Section 10 deems delivery effective when
a message becomes available rather than when it is read, transferring the legal
consequence of non-engagement to the recipient. Exemption exists, but only against
statutory criteria (Table 1).

The mandate rode on a single national identity credential: NemID, replaced by
MitID from October 2021, required for borger.dk, skat.dk and sundhed.dk from
22 September 2022, and withdrawn entirely on 31 October 2023. That pairing is the
policy's real design. A mandate alone moves letters; a mandate plus one universal
credential builds a *rail* — an authentication layer every Dane must hold and
every firm may build on without financing its adoption. Rails are innovation
infrastructure, because they lower the fixed cost of everything constructed on top
of them.

The compulsion is also why the adoption statistics that follow must be read
carefully. After 1 November 2014, enrolment measures compliance with a legal
obligation, not revealed demand. That distinction governs this report.

The programme was justified on a number. The Ministry of Finance business case
projected savings of approximately DKK 1,000 million per year from mandatory
Digital Post — the return that warranted compelling roughly 5.07 million people.
Section 6 returns to how much of it survived audit.

## 2. Adoption and economic effect

Mandatory Digital Post (Lov om Offentlig Digital Post, LOV nr 528 af 11/06/2012;
compulsory for businesses from 2013 and citizens from 1 November 2014) did more than move correspondence online. With the single national identity
credential that carries it, it built a *rail*: a universal, legally guaranteed
authentication layer firms could build on without financing adoption themselves. The economic question is therefore
not whether Danes use digital services — compulsion settles that — but what the rail let
others build, and where that building stopped.

Banking shows the magnitude. Danish retail bank branches fell from 2,025 in 2004 to
665 in 2024, a decline of 67.2% (Figure 1). Read alone, it suggests
digitalisation destroyed banking employment. The longer series refuses
it. Between 1991 and 2024 the number of financial institutions fell by 76.7%, from
219 to 51, while bank employment fell by only 29.4%, from roughly 51,000 to 36,000
(Figure 9). Staff per surviving institution therefore rose from 233 to 706, an
increase of 203%. Consolidation concentrated the sector rather than shedding it. The branch series begins in 2004, so its −67.2%
is measured over a different window from the other two and the three changes must
not be differenced against one another.

Payments moved with it. Cash fell from 23% to 9% of the *number* of payments in
physical retail between 2017 and 2025 — 14 percentage points, to under two-fifths of
its 2017 share — while card-based mobile wallets reached 32% of that same measure
(full series in the accompanying workbook, sheet F2_PAYMENTS). Much of this is
displacement within digital instruments, not cash vanishing: wallet
payments are card payments, and the physical card share fell from 73% to 54% across
the same period.

Does wider consumer adoption accompany greater commercial activity? Figure 6 tests
this across the European Union in 2024, plotting the share of internet users who
purchased online against the share of enterprise turnover from e-sales.
Across the 18 member states holding both measures, the fitted slope is +0.602
percentage points of enterprise turnover per percentage point of consumer adoption
(standard error 0.105; t = 5.75 on 16 degrees of freedom; 95% confidence interval
[0.380, 0.824]; R² = 0.674).

Two features make the estimate more credible than its sample size suggests. An
earlier version of this figure drew its adoption values from a Eurostat news
release, which named only the three highest member states, the three lowest and two movers; on those six tail countries the fit was R² 0.853 with a slope of
+0.655. Replacing them with the complete databrowser extract moved the slope by
about 8% while the fit fell by 0.18 — the signature of selection on the tails,
measured rather than asserted. Second, the nine member states dropped
for want of the turnover measure average 75.67% on adoption against the plotted
eighteen's 77.23%, a difference not statistically distinguishable (p ≈ 0.71).
They are absent through data availability, not selection on the axis.

Causation is neither established nor establishable from this design. Adoption
measures consumers; turnover measures enterprises, including business-to-business
ordering no consumer touches. Both plausibly rise with national income, which
appears on neither axis. Denmark sits above the fitted line — but by less than
one residual standard error, and behind three other member states. It is consistent
with the European pattern, not exceptional to it.

## 3. Diffusion: Denmark against the European Union

"Breadth" has two subjects in Denmark and they point opposite ways. Citizen
breadth is near-complete: 90.86% of Danish internet users bought online in 2024,
third of the twenty-seven member states (full ranking in the workbook, sheet
F5_EU27). Enterprise breadth is not. Only 38.78% of Danish enterprises make any
e-sales at all — second-highest in the Union, behind Lithuania's 43.03%, and still
under two firms in five. Denmark leads Europe at a level most Danish firms have
not reached.

Intensity moved where breadth did not. Among firms that do sell online, e-sales
rose from 17.05% of enterprise turnover in 2014 to 33.31% in 2024, while the EU-27
average rose from 16.43% to 19.49% (Figure 3). Denmark and the Union began the
decade level; Denmark roughly doubled and the EU gained three percentage points.
Depth without breadth, stated precisely.

The same pattern governs the newest technology, and it identifies who could build
on the rail. In 2025, 42.03% of Danish enterprises used artificial intelligence —
but 74.52% of large firms against 40.99% of small and medium-sized ones, a gap of
33.53 percentage points (Figure 7). This is not a connectivity or literacy failure:
92.45% of Danish SMEs clear the basic digital-intensity threshold, against an EU
average of 71.39%. Danish SMEs are connected, capable and on the rail. What they
lack is the complementary capital — data, integration, specialist staff — that
turns access into application. Compulsion built the infrastructure; it could not
distribute the capacity to exploit it.

Nor did it guarantee quality. Denmark scores 82.2 on the Digital Decade measure of
digital public services for citizens, below the EU average of 84.64. The country
with Europe's most compulsory digital government delivers slightly below-average
services through it.

This maps directly onto Sustainable Development Goal 8, and specifically Target
8.2 — higher productivity through technological upgrading and innovation.
Denmark's constraint on 8.2 is not adoption but diffusion: the productivity gain
is concentrated in the firms that were already best placed to capture it.

## 4. Who the rail left behind

Start where the mandate bites hardest. Among Danish citizens aged 75–84, 20% hold
a formal exemption from Digital Post; among those aged 85 and over, 33% do. One in
three of the oldest cohort sits legally outside a system the state made
compulsory. These are 2022 figures and the headline rate below is from 2026, so
they are complementary measures of the same exclusion, not points on a trend.

Against that, the aggregate looks small. In the first quarter of 2026, 4.7% of
citizens — 238,479 people — were exempt, implying a mandated base of about
5.07 million. The aggregate is small because the affected
population is small, not because exclusion is rare where it lands.

Exemption is also the narrowest available measure. Figure 4 sets it against four
wider estimates of the same problem: 6.5% do not use digital public
services at all; 16.5% report difficulty using them; the Agency for Digital
Government describes 17–22% of the population as digitally disadvantaged; and
Justitia puts up to 25% of adults in that category. The relief mechanism reaches
4.7%. The lowest credible estimate of need is 16.5%. The 11.8-percentage-point
difference is roughly 600,000 citizens who struggle with a compulsory system and
hold no formal standing outside it.

Weak digital skills would be the convenient explanation; the data refuse it.
Denmark's weakest age band, 55–74, reaches 67.81% with at least
basic digital skills against an EU average of 42.60% for that band. Denmark leads the EU in all three age bands, and its
internal gradient from youngest to oldest is 24.25 percentage points against the
EU's 31.95 (Figure 10). Denmark is both the best-skilled and the flattest distribution in the Union.

The exclusion is therefore not a capability deficit but an administrative one. The
mandate was calibrated above the bottom of its own distribution, and the exemption
criteria were drawn narrower than the difficulty they were meant to relieve. A rail
that everybody must board and a fifth of the population finds hard to use is not a
universal rail; it is a universal obligation with a private cost attached, and that
cost falls on the citizens least able to build anything on top of it.

## 6. Recommendation and limitations

The report opened on a projection of roughly DKK 1,000 million a year. Rigsrevisionen
could verify DKK 450 million of it — postage, paper and envelopes. The wage and
overhead component was never substantiated, and the cross-government study intended
to settle it was abandoned when the municipalities declined to take part. Denmark
compelled 5.07 million people on a business case that remains 55% unaudited.

Two implications follow. First, the benefit side of digital mandates should be
audited as rigorously as the cost side before compulsion is extended. Second, the
diffusion gap is the higher-return target: SMV:Digital has supported roughly 7,000
SME projects, of which 65% of participants report investing further (Figure 8).
That figure is self-reported and has no control group, so it is a measure of
participation, not of effect — which is precisely the evaluation the scheme still
needs. The same discipline applies here: the cross-section in Figure 6 has
eighteen observations and establishes association, not cause.
