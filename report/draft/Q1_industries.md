## Question 1. Digital innovation and industry transformation

Denmark's digital transformation began with an obligation rather than an
invitation, and that origin explains both its speed and its shape. *Lov om
Offentlig Digital Post* [[RI1]] made a state-run digital mailbox the default
legal channel for public correspondence, binding businesses from 2013 and every
citizen aged 15 and over from 1 November 2014. Alongside it came a universal
electronic credential, dated in Table 1.

The combination is what distinguishes the Danish case. A mandate alone changes
how letters are sent. A mandate joined to a universal credential creates
authentication infrastructure that every citizen must hold and any firm may build upon without financing its adoption. That lowers the fixed cost of every service built on top of it. As an adoption instrument it worked: Eurostat
[[ES9:y]] records that 98.5% of Danish individuals aged 16 to 74 used a public
authority website or app during 2024, the highest share in the Union against an
EU-27 average of 70%. Because compulsion accounts for that use, the economic interest lies not in the
adoption but in what private industry built on the same foundation. Two sectors
show the pattern.

Retail banking consolidated rather than contracted. Finans Danmark [[FD1:y]]
reports that branches fell from 2,025 in 2004 to 665 in 2024, a decline of 67%
([[F1]]). Taken alone that would suggest digitalisation destroyed banking employment, but
the longer series qualifies it. Between 1991 and 2024 institutions fell by 77%,
from 219 to 51, while employment fell by only 29%, from roughly 51,000 to 36,000
([[F9]]), so staff per surviving institution rose from 233 to 706. The
technologies behind this are online and mobile banking authenticated by MitID.
Once identity and correspondence moved onto shared national infrastructure, the
branch ceased to be the distribution channel, output per institution rose, and
the sector shed premises rather than labour.

Retail payments show innovation of a different kind. Danmarks Nationalbank
[[NB1:y]] reports that cash fell from 23% to 9% of the number of payments made
in physical retail between 2017 and 2025, while card-based mobile wallets
reached 32% of the same measure ([[F2]]). Much of this is displacement within digital instruments rather than fresh
digitalisation, because a wallet payment is a card payment and the physical card
share fell from 73% to 54%. What changed was the interface rather than the rails beneath it, the kind of
incremental innovation that shared infrastructure makes cheap to attempt.

Whether wider consumer adoption is associated with greater commercial activity
can be tested directly. Using Eurostat [[ES7:y]], [[F6]] plots the share of
internet users purchasing online against e-sales as a share of enterprise
turnover across the Union in 2024, turnover from a separate extract [[ES6]]. Across the 18 member states reporting both, the fitted
slope is +0.60 percentage points of turnover per percentage point of adoption,
with an R² of 0.674 and diagnostics in [[AP:regression]]. Causation is not
established: adoption is measured on consumers and turnover on enterprises, and
national income appears on neither axis. Denmark sits above the line by less than one residual standard error, within
the European pattern rather than outside it.
