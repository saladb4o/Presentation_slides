## Question 1. Digital innovation and industry transformation

Denmark's digital transformation began with an obligation rather than an
invitation, and that origin explains its shape. *Lov om
Offentlig Digital Post* [[RI1]] made a state-run digital mailbox the default
legal channel for public correspondence, binding businesses from 2013 and every
citizen aged 15 and over from 1 November 2014. Alongside it the state issued a
universal electronic credential, whose dates Table 1 records.

What distinguishes the Danish case is the combination. A mandate alone changes
how letters are sent; joined to a universal credential, it creates
authentication infrastructure every citizen must hold and any firm may build on
without financing its adoption. As an adoption
instrument it worked: Eurostat [[ES9:y]] records that 98.5% of Danish
individuals aged 16 to 74 used a public authority website or app during 2024,
the highest share in the Union against an EU-27 average of 70%. Because
compulsion rather than preference accounts for that use, the economic interest
lies in what industry built on it.

In retail banking the outcome was consolidation rather than contraction.
Finans Danmark [[FD1:y]] reports that branches fell from 2,025 in 2004 to 665 in
2024, a decline of 67% ([[F1]]), which alone suggests that digitalisation
destroyed banking employment. The longer series qualifies that reading: between
1991 and 2024 institutions fell by 77% while employment fell by only 29%
([[F9]]), so staff per surviving institution rose from 233 to 706. Once identity
and correspondence moved onto shared national infrastructure, authenticated by
MitID, the branch ceased to be the distribution channel: the sector shed
premises rather than labour.

Retail payments show innovation of a different kind. Danmarks Nationalbank
[[NB1:y]] reports that cash fell from 23% to 9% of the number of payments made
in physical retail between 2017 and 2025, while card-based mobile wallets
reached 32% of the same measure ([[F2]]). Much of this is displacement within
digital instruments rather than fresh digitalisation, since a wallet payment is
itself a card payment, and the physical card share fell from 73% to 54%.

Whether wider adoption accompanies greater commercial activity can be tested.
Using Eurostat [[ES7:y]], [[F6]] plots the share of internet users purchasing
online against e-sales as a share of enterprise turnover across the Union in
2024, turnover from a separate extract [[ES6]]. Across the 18 member
states reporting both, the fitted slope is +0.60 percentage points of turnover
per percentage point of adoption, with an R² of 0.674 and diagnostics in
[[AP:regression]]. Causation is not established: adoption is measured on consumers,
turnover on enterprises, and national income on neither. Denmark sits above the
line by less than one residual standard error, within the European pattern.
