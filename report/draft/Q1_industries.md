## Question 1. Digital innovation and industry transformation

Denmark did not persuade its citizens to adopt digital public services; it
required them to do so. *Lov om Offentlig Digital Post* [[RI1]] made a state-run
digital mailbox the default legal channel for public correspondence, binding
businesses from 2013 and every citizen aged 15 and over from 1 November 2014.
Digitaliseringsstyrelsen [[DG4:y]] records that the accompanying identity
credential, NemID, was replaced by MitID from October 2021 and withdrawn on 31
October 2023. The combination is what distinguishes the Danish case. A
mandate alone changes how letters are sent; a mandate plus a universal credential
establishes shared authentication infrastructure that every citizen must hold and
any firm may build upon without financing its adoption, lowering the fixed cost of
every service developed on it. Because compulsion accounts for citizens' use, the
economic interest lies in what others built on that foundation.

**Retail banking.** Finans Danmark [[FD1:y]] reports that Danish retail bank
branches fell from 2,025 in 2004 to 665 in 2024, a decline of 67.2% ([[F1]]).
Taken alone that suggests digitalisation eliminated banking employment, but the
longer series qualifies it. Between 1991 and 2024 the number of financial
institutions fell by 76.7%, from 219 to 51, while bank employment fell by only
29.4%, from roughly 51,000 to 36,000 ([[F9]]). Staff per surviving institution
therefore rose from 233 to 706, an increase of 203%. The business model changed rather than the
workforce: with authentication and correspondence on shared national
infrastructure, branch networks became redundant as a distribution channel and
the sector consolidated into fewer and larger institutions. The three series
begin in different years and must not be differenced.

**Retail payments.** Danmarks Nationalbank [[NB1:y]] reports that cash fell from
23% to 9% of the *number* of payments in physical retail between 2017 and 2025,
while card-based mobile wallets reached 32% of the same measure ([[F2]]). Much of
this is displacement within digital instruments rather than new digitalisation,
because wallet payments are card payments and the physical card share fell from
73% to 54%. What changed for consumers was the interface, not the rails
underneath, which is precisely the kind of innovation that shared infrastructure
makes cheap to attempt. Cash acceptance nonetheless remains a statutory
obligation under *lov om betalinger* §81 [[RI2]].

**Broader economic effect.** Whether wider consumer adoption is associated with
greater commercial activity can be tested directly. Using Eurostat data
[[ES7:y]], [[F6]] plots the share of internet users purchasing online against
e-sales as a share of enterprise turnover across the European Union in 2024, the
turnover measure from a separate extract [[ES6]]. Across the 18 member states
reporting both, the fitted slope is +0.602 percentage points of turnover per
percentage point of adoption, with a standard error of 0.105 and an R² of 0.674.
[[AP:regression]] reports the diagnostics. Causation is not established: adoption is
measured on consumers and turnover on enterprises, and national income appears on
neither axis. Denmark sits above the fitted line by less than one residual
standard error and behind three other states, consistent with the European
pattern rather than exceptional to it.
