## Question 1. Digital innovation and industry transformation

Denmark did not persuade its citizens to adopt digital public services; it
required them to do so. *Lov om Offentlig Digital Post* [[RI1]] made a state-run
digital mailbox the default legal channel for public correspondence, binding
businesses from 2013 and every citizen aged 15 and over from 1 November 2014.
Table 1 dates the accompanying credential: NemID gave way to MitID, withdrawn on
31 October 2023. The combination is what distinguishes the Danish case. A mandate alone changes
how letters are sent; a mandate plus a universal credential creates shared
authentication infrastructure every citizen must hold and any firm may build on
without financing its adoption, lowering the fixed cost of services built on
it. The mandate worked as an adoption instrument: Eurostat [[ES9:y]] records
that 98.5% of Danish individuals aged 16 to 74 used a public authority website
or app during 2024, the highest share in the Union against an EU-27 average of
70.0%. Because compulsion accounts for that use, the economic interest lies in
what others built on the same infrastructure.

**Retail banking.** Finans Danmark [[FD1:y]] reports that Danish retail bank
branches fell from 2,025 in 2004 to 665 in 2024, a decline of 67.2% ([[F1]]).
Taken alone that suggests digitalisation eliminated banking employment, but the
longer series qualifies it: between 1991 and 2024 financial institutions fell
76.7%, from 219 to 51, while bank employment fell only 29.4%, from roughly
51,000 to 36,000 ([[F9]]). Staff per surviving institution rose from 233 to 706. The technologies doing this are online and mobile banking authenticated by
MitID: with identity verification and correspondence on shared national
infrastructure, branches ceased to be the distribution channel, output per
institution rose and the sector consolidated rather than shed labour. The three series
begin in different years and must not be differenced.

**Retail payments.** Danmarks Nationalbank [[NB1:y]] reports that cash fell from
23% to 9% of the *number* of payments in physical retail between 2017 and 2025,
while card-based mobile wallets reached 32% of the same measure ([[F2]]). Much
of this is displacement within digital instruments rather than new
digitalisation, because wallet payments are card payments and the physical card
share fell from 73% to 54%. What changed was the interface, not the rails
underneath, which is the kind of innovation shared infrastructure makes cheap to
attempt.

**Broader economic effect.** Whether wider consumer adoption is associated with
greater commercial activity can be tested directly. Using Eurostat [[ES7:y]],
[[F6]] plots the share of internet users purchasing online against e-sales as a
share of enterprise turnover across the Union in 2024, the turnover measure from
a separate extract [[ES6]]. Across the 18 member states
reporting both, the fitted slope is +0.602 percentage points of turnover per
percentage point of adoption, with a standard error of 0.105 and an R² of 0.674.
[[AP:regression]] reports the diagnostics. Causation is not established:
adoption is measured on consumers and turnover on enterprises, and national
income appears on neither axis. Denmark sits above the
line by less than one residual standard error and behind three other states,
consistent with the European pattern rather than exceptional to it.
