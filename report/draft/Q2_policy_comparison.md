## Question 2. Policy comparison: SME digitalisation support in Denmark and Germany

**The problem both policies address.** Denmark leads Europe on citizen adoption
but not on firm capability. Its citizens rank first in the Union on e-government
use; its firms rank second on e-sales and still only 38.78% of them make any,
behind Lithuania at 43.03% and under two firms in five [[ES5]]. A high rank on a
low level is the problem, not the achievement. Intensity rose where breadth did not: e-sales grew from 17.05% of enterprise
turnover in 2014 to 33.31% in 2024, against an EU-27 average moving from 16.43%
to 19.49% ([[F3]]). The same split governs the newest technology. The European Commission
[[EC1:y]] reports that 42.03% of Danish enterprises used artificial intelligence
in 2025, comprising 74.52% of large firms against 40.99% of small and medium
ones, a gap of 33.53 percentage points ([[F7]]). This is not a connectivity failure, since 92.45% of Danish SMEs clear the basic
digital-intensity threshold against an EU average of 71.39%. What smaller firms
lack is the complementary capital, namely data, systems integration and
specialist staff, that converts access into application.

**The two instruments.** Denmark's SMV:Digital and Germany's *Digital Jetzt*
share an objective, to raise digital investment among firms that would not
otherwise make it, and both are co-financed grants to small and medium
enterprises. They differ in how they choose recipients. SMV:Digital scores applications and
funds the highest scorers until the pool is exhausted, the next pool opening on
26 October 2026 [[SMV1]]; *Digital Jetzt* allocated by lottery among eligible
applicants.

**Effectiveness.** Denmark's scheme is the better evidenced.
Digitaliseringsstyrelsen [[DG3:y]] links recipients to Danmarks Statistik
registers and compares them with matched controls, reporting revenue growth of
18% against 13% and employment growth of 8% against 4%. No comparable
register-linked estimate is published for *Digital Jetzt*, and its allocation
rule drew an objection Denmark's does not face: the Bundesrechnungshof [[BRH1:y]]
criticised awarding the funding by lottery rather than on criteria, warning that
firms would postpone investment while waiting for a favourable draw, and called
for the procedure to be discontinued. A lottery makes the grant a prize worth
waiting for, and waiting is the opposite of what the scheme exists to produce.

**Weaknesses and implementation challenges.** Denmark's advantage is narrower
than it appears. Matching addresses selection on what the registers record,
namely sector, size, age and prior trajectory, and not on what they do not.
Selection into a voluntary scheme runs on managerial ambition and the slack to
write an application, the traits that also produce revenue growth, so the matched
estimate retains an upward bias of unknown size. Neither scheme reaches firms
that never apply.

**Recommended improvement.** SMV:Digital already scores applicants, so it already
produces a funding threshold; it simply discards the information. Near the
cut-off, whether a firm is funded turns on assessor variation rather than on the
firm, which makes applicants just above and just below comparable on exactly the
unobservables that defeat matching. Retaining the scores and publishing the
cut-off would allow a regression discontinuity estimate at administrative cost
only ([[AP:policy_spec]]).
