## Question 2. Policy comparison: SME digitalisation support in Denmark and Germany

**The problem both policies address.** Denmark leads Europe on citizen adoption
and not on firm capability. Eurostat [[ES7:y]] records that 90.86% of Danish
internet users bought online in 2024, third of the twenty-seven member states,
while only 38.78% of Danish enterprises make any e-sales, second in the Union
behind Lithuania at 43.03% and still under two firms in five [[ES5]]. Intensity
rose where breadth did not: e-sales grew from 17.05% of enterprise turnover in
2014 to 33.31% in 2024, against an EU-27 average moving from 16.43% to 19.49%
([[F3]]). The same split governs the newest technology. The European Commission
[[EC1:y]] reports that 42.03% of Danish enterprises used artificial intelligence
in 2025, comprising 74.52% of large firms against 40.99% of small and medium
ones, a gap of 33.53 percentage points ([[F7]]). This is not a connectivity
failure, since 92.45% of Danish SMEs clear the basic digital-intensity threshold
against an EU average of 71.39%. What smaller firms lack is the complementary
capital, namely data, systems integration and specialist staff, that converts
access into application.

**The two instruments.** Denmark's SMV:Digital and Germany's *Digital Jetzt*
share an objective, which is to raise digital investment among firms that would
not otherwise make it, and both operate as co-financed grants to small and
medium enterprises. They differ in how they choose recipients. SMV:Digital scores applications and
funds the highest scorers until the pool is exhausted, the next pool opening on
26 October 2026 [[SMV1]]; *Digital Jetzt* allocated by lottery among eligible
applicants.

**Effectiveness.** Denmark's scheme is the better evidenced of the two.
Digitaliseringsstyrelsen [[DG3:y]] links recipients to Danmarks Statistik
registers and compares them with matched control firms, reporting revenue growth
of 18% against the controls' 13% and employment growth of 8% against 4%. Germany
has no comparable estimate, and its allocation rule produced a perverse result:
the Bundesrechnungshof [[BRH1:y]] found that firms postponed investment while
waiting for a favourable draw and recommended that the procedure be discontinued.
A lottery makes the grant a prize worth waiting for, and waiting is the opposite
of the behaviour the scheme exists to produce.

**Weaknesses and implementation challenges.** Denmark's advantage is narrower
than it appears. Matching addresses selection on what the registers record,
namely sector, size, age and prior trajectory, and not on what they do not.
Selection into a voluntary scheme runs on managerial ambition, an existing
digitalisation plan and the slack to write an application, which are the traits
that also produce revenue growth. The matched estimate therefore retains an
upward bias of unknown size, and neither scheme reaches firms that never apply.

**Recommended improvement.** SMV:Digital already scores applicants, so it
already produces a funding threshold; it simply discards the information. Near
the cut-off, whether a firm is funded turns on assessor variation rather than on
the firm, which makes applicants just above and just below comparable on exactly
the unobservables that defeat matching. Retaining the scores and publishing the
cut-off would allow a regression discontinuity estimate at administrative cost
only, with no change to who receives money. [[AP:policy_spec]] specifies the
design.
