## Question 5. Guest speaker reflection: AI, sustainability and the Danish digital economy

The session addressed corporate sustainability at PwC rather than digital
government, and the connection to Denmark was not one I expected. It rests on an
idea running through both halves of the talk: adoption is easy to claim and
effect is hard to prove, so what separates them is whether somebody other than
the claimant does the measuring.

Tran [[GS1:y]] reported that in a PwC survey of Asia-Pacific chief executives,
82% had adopted generative artificial intelligence within five years while far
fewer could identify revenue attributable to it. Denmark shows the same gap in
its own data. Adoption is high by European standards at 42.03% of enterprises,
and it is concentrated: 74.52% of large firms against 40.99% of small and medium
ones. Access has been distributed and capability has not, which is the finding
Question 2 reaches from Danish evidence and the guest speaker reached from a
regional survey.

The sustainability half supplied the test. Tran's position was that a corporate
net zero commitment means little unless an external body has validated it,
because an unvalidated commitment is one the claimant grades itself; PwC's own
reduction of 22% against a 2019 baseline is stated against a target the Science
Based Targets initiative validated. Applied to Denmark, that standard is
uncomfortable. SMV:Digital's headline figures are reported by participants to
the body funding the scheme [[DG3]], which is why Question 2 recommends an
evaluation the agency does not run. Denmark's position on ICT waste recovery is
measured and published by the European Commission and has not produced a
corresponding target, which is disclosure without commitment rather than the
reverse.

One detail transfers directly to policy. Tran put the cost of that validation at
USD 10,000 to 25,000, which in practice restricts it to large corporations, and
described publicly funded programmes that extend equivalent capability to
smaller firms. That is the same instrument, and the same justification, as
SMV:Digital. A fixed cost of proving anything, whether emissions or returns, is
heavier the smaller the firm bearing it, and closing that gap is a government
function in both domains.
